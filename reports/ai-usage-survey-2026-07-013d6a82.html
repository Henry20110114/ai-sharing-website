中学生 AI 使用情况调研：42 份回答里的发现How students use AI: findings from 42 survey responses

2026 年 7 月，我们向身边的中学生发起了一次关于 AI 使用情况的问卷调查。这里是结果的整理，供学生、老师和家长参考。In July 2026 we surveyed secondary-school students we know about how they use AI. Here is what 42 of them told us, for students, teachers, and parents.

42份回答，2026 年 7 月 9–13 日收集
93%每周至少使用 AI 辅助学习几次
74%曾把至少一项学习任务几乎完全交给 AI
26%非常清楚学校或老师对 AI 使用的要求

主要发现

  • AI 已经是日常学习工具。93% 的同学每周至少使用几次，36% 每天使用。最常用的是豆包（79%）、ChatGPT（64%）和 DeepSeek（50%），平均每人用过约 2.6 种服务。
  • 主要用来理解和查找，但「整个交出去」也不少见。使用最多的环节是理解概念（67%）、查找资料（67%）和解题思路（57%）。同时，74% 的同学曾把至少一项任务几乎完全交给 AI：26% 让 AI 解答过完整题目，21% 让 AI 写过整篇作文或作业。假期作业赶不完时，64% 表示会用 AI 直接生成答案，至少作为应急。
  • 核查习惯参差不齐。60% 的同学总是或经常核查 AI 的答案，40% 只是偶尔或更少。同学们自己也意识到这一点：最希望学校教的正是「如何判断 AI 答案是否可靠」（57%）。
  • 效率明显提高，但一部分人感到了依赖。79% 认为 AI 对学习方式影响较大或很大，81% 觉得获取资料更快，64% 觉得学习动力有所增强。同时 19% 承认更依赖 AI，17% 觉得独立思考减少；假如一段时间不能用 AI，33% 预计自己会独立思考变多。
  • 希望老师留在课堂里。对「纯 AI 教学」，88% 的同学认为 AI 只能辅助，或者需要教师监督。
  • 学校的指导还不够清楚。只有 26% 非常清楚学校或老师对 AI 使用的要求，36% 不清楚、完全不了解，或者表示学校没有明确要求。

谁回答了问卷

共收到 42 份回答，来自 2026 年 7 月 9 日至 13 日。男生 28 人（67%），女生 13 人（31%），1 人不便透露。年级以高一为主，课程体系以体制内为主，也有不少 A-Level、AP 和 IB 的同学。

样本不大，也不是随机抽样，所以下面的百分比只描述这 42 位同学的情况，不能推广到所有中学生。详见文末的说明。

用得有多频繁，用什么

AI 辅助学习在这群同学里已经是常态：只有 3 人表示很少使用。

通用问答类 AI 几乎人人都用（88%）。翻译、搜索整理和写作润色类各有四成左右的人在用，专门的编程类 AI 只有 12%。

豆包是使用最广的服务，ChatGPT 和 DeepSeek 紧随其后。多数同学同时使用不止一种：42 人共提到 111 次服务，平均每人约 2.6 种。

用在哪些学习环节

使用最多的是理解概念和查找资料，其次是解题思路和翻译。这些更接近「帮我搞懂」，而不是「帮我做完」。

不过，「帮我做完」也并不少见。除了 11 位从未这样做的同学，其余 31 人（74%）都曾把至少一项任务几乎完全交给 AI，最常见的是查资料整理，其次是解答完整题目和制作 PPT 或项目文案。

问卷设计了一个具体情境：假期作业很多，明天开学，今晚做不完。只有 2 人明确表示不会用 AI 生成答案；43% 会把它当作临时应急，21% 表示经常这样做。

写作类作业的使用方式比较一致：超过一半的同学让 AI 提供大纲和素材方向，正文自己写。让 AI 直接生成全文的和完全不用 AI 的都是少数。

在研学或小组项目里，AI 主要用于查资料、构思方向和润色文字。直接让 AI 完成自己负责部分的有 6 人（14%）。

查证与信息素养

遇到陌生概念时，四成同学会先用搜索引擎再问 AI，36% 直接问 AI，19% 先看课本。先问老师或同学的只有 1 人。

绝大多数同学（95%）认为「手动」搜索和整理资料的技能仍然需要，但过半认为它的重要性在降低。

核查 AI 答案的习惯差异很大：总是核查的 31%，经常核查的 29%，剩下四成只是偶尔、很少或从不核查。

对学习的影响

近八成同学认为 AI 对自己的学习方式影响较大或很大。

影响主要体现在效率上：获取资料更快、复习效率提高。也有一部分同学报告了另一面：更依赖 AI（19%）、独立思考减少（17%）。

学习动力方面，64% 认为有所增强，31% 认为基本没有影响，只有 1 人认为明显削弱。

如果一段时间不能使用 AI，同学们预计最大的变化是查资料更费时间。值得注意的是，三分之一的人预计自己会独立思考变多。

对 AI 的看法

对「纯 AI 教学模式」，同学们并不激进：45% 认为 AI 只能辅助教学，43% 比较看好但需要教师监督，只有 5 人认为可以替代部分课堂。

关于知识产权，近三分之二认为 AI 会构成威胁，但其中大部分认为可以通过规则管理。

对创造力的影响，最多的看法是「短期有帮助，但可能形成依赖」（43%）。认为会提供更多灵感的（21%）和认为会限制原创能力的（19%）大致相当。

使用偏好

多数同学希望 AI 先简略回答、需要时再展开，或者给出简短结论加关键理由；只有 19% 希望一上来就分步骤详细解释。

当回答太长时，最常见的做法是让 AI 重新概括，或者视重要程度决定看多少。

学校的指导

关于学校或老师对 AI 使用的要求，只有 26% 的同学非常清楚。14% 完全不了解，10% 表示学校目前没有明确要求。

同学们最希望得到的指导集中在三件事上：如何判断 AI 答案是否可靠、不同学科中怎么用、如何向 AI 提问。学术诚信和写作引用规范也各有四成同学希望了解。只有 2 人认为不需要指导。

一些建议

给同学

  • 把「先给思路，不给答案」当作默认的提问方式。多数人在作文上已经这样做了，题目和作业也可以。
  • 重要的内容一定核查。AI 会很自信地出错，数据、引文、公式和历史事实尤其要对照可靠来源。
  • 保留手动搜索和整理资料的能力。95% 的同学认为它仍然需要；在正式的学习和研究中，它就是基本功。
  • 留意自己的依赖感。如果发现关掉 AI 就不会做了，说明用法需要调整。

给老师和学校

  • 把要求写清楚。超过三分之一的同学不清楚学校的规定，明确的规则比含糊的禁止更有用。
  • 直接教「怎么判断答案是否可靠」和「怎么提问」。这是同学们提出的最迫切的两项需求。
  • 按学科给出具体的使用方法，而不是笼统地允许或禁止。
  • 作业设计可以默认 AI 的存在，比如要求提交大纲、思路和核查过程。

给家长

  • AI 已经是孩子学习的日常工具，主要用于理解和查资料，但也会用来赶作业。
  • 比起禁止，更有用的是聊一聊：用它做什么，有没有核查，离开它还会不会。

关于这次调研

  • 问卷通过 Microsoft Forms 在 2026 年 7 月 9 日至 13 日收集，共 42 份回答。
  • 受访者是通过身边关系邀请的中学生，不是随机抽样，样本以高一男生为主。因此所有百分比只描述这 42 人，不代表更大的群体。
  • 所有回答均为自我报告。
  • 多选题的百分比是选择该项的人数占全部受访者的比例，各项相加会超过 100%。
  • 年级一栏是自由填写的，报告中按 7–9 年级和 10、11、12 年级归并；2 份无法判断的记为「未注明」。

Key findings

  • AI is an everyday study tool. 93% use it at least a few times a week and 36% daily. Doubao (79%), ChatGPT (64%), and DeepSeek (50%) lead; students use about 2.6 services each on average.
  • Mostly for understanding and finding things, but handing over whole tasks is common too. The top uses are understanding concepts (67%), finding information (67%), and working out how to solve problems (57%). At the same time, 74% have handed at least one task almost entirely to AI: 26% have had it solve entire problems, and 21% have had it write a whole essay or assignment. Facing a pile of unfinished holiday homework, 64% would have AI generate the answers, at least as a last resort.
  • Checking habits are uneven. 60% always or often check AI's answers; 40% only sometimes or less. Students know it: the guidance they most want from school is “how to judge whether AI answers are reliable” (57%).
  • Big efficiency gains, and some sense of dependence. 79% say AI has changed how they learn a lot; 81% get information faster; 64% feel more motivated. But 19% admit they've become more dependent on AI, 17% say they think less independently, and 33% expect they'd think more independently if they couldn't use AI for a while.
  • They want teachers in the room. On a “pure AI” teaching model, 88% say AI can only assist, or that teacher supervision is needed.
  • School guidance is thin. Only 26% are very clear about their school's rules on AI; 36% are unclear, unaware, or say there are no clear rules.

Who answered

42 responses came in between 9 and 13 July 2026: 28 boys (67%), 13 girls (31%), and one who preferred not to say. Most are in Grade 10 and most follow the Chinese national curriculum, with a good number of A-Level, AP, and IB students.

The sample is small and not random, so the percentages below describe these 42 students only and can't be generalized to all secondary-school students. See the notes at the end.

How often, and which tools

Using AI for learning is the norm in this group: only 3 students said they rarely use it.

General chat assistants are near-universal (88%). Translation, search/research, and writing tools are each used by around four in ten; dedicated coding assistants by only 12%.

Doubao is the most widely used service, followed by ChatGPT and DeepSeek. Most students use more than one: 42 students named services 111 times in total, about 2.6 each.

Where AI is used

The most common uses are understanding concepts and finding information, followed by working out how to solve problems and translation. These are closer to “help me understand” than “do it for me”.

“Do it for me” is not rare, though. Apart from the 11 students who have never done it, the other 31 (74%) have handed at least one task almost entirely to AI, most often research and organizing, then solving entire problems and making slides or project write-ups.

The survey posed a concrete scenario: lots of holiday homework, school starts tomorrow, and it can't all be finished tonight. Only 2 students said they wouldn't have AI generate the answers; 43% would use it as a last resort, and 21% said they often do.

For writing assignments the pattern is consistent: more than half have AI provide an outline and ideas and write the text themselves. Having AI write the whole thing, and not using AI at all, are both minorities.

In field studies and group projects, AI is used mainly for research, brainstorming the direction, and polishing text. Six students (14%) would have it do their whole part.

Checking and information literacy

Faced with an unfamiliar concept, four in ten search first and then ask AI, 36% ask AI directly, and 19% start with the textbook. Only one student asks a teacher or classmate first.

Almost everyone (95%) thinks the “manual” skills of searching and organizing information are still needed, but more than half think they matter less than before.

Checking AI's answers varies a lot: 31% always check, 29% often, and the remaining four in ten only sometimes, rarely, or never.

Effect on learning

Nearly eight in ten say AI has changed the way they learn a lot.

The change is mostly about efficiency: faster access to information and more efficient revision. Some also report the other side: more dependent on AI (19%), less independent thinking (17%).

On motivation, 64% say it is stronger, 31% see no real effect, and just one student says it is clearly weaker.

If they couldn't use AI for a while, the biggest expected change is that research would take longer. Notably, a third expect they would think more independently.

Views on AI

On a “pure AI” teaching model, students are not radical: 45% think AI can only assist teaching, 43% are fairly optimistic but want teacher supervision, and only 5 think it could replace some classes.

On intellectual property, nearly two-thirds see AI as a threat, but most of those think it can be managed with rules.

On creativity, the most common view is that heavy AI use “helps short-term but may create dependence” (43%). Those who think it gives more inspiration (21%) and those who think it limits originality (19%) are roughly equal.

Preferences

Most students want a brief answer first with the option to expand, or a short conclusion with key reasons; only 19% want a detailed step-by-step explanation up front.

When an answer is too long, the most common responses are to ask AI to summarize, or to decide how much to read by how important it is.

School guidance

Only 26% are very clear about their school's or teachers' rules on AI use. 14% are not aware of any, and 10% say their school has no clear rules yet.

The guidance students want most comes down to three things: how to judge whether AI answers are reliable, how to use AI in different subjects, and how to ask good questions. Academic honesty and writing/citation rules each interest four in ten. Only 2 students said they need no guidance.

Suggestions

For students

  • Make “hints first, not answers” your default way of asking. Most of you already do this for essays; it works for problems and homework too.
  • Always check what matters. AI makes mistakes with complete confidence; numbers, quotations, formulas, and historical facts especially need a reliable source.
  • Keep the ability to search and organize information yourself. 95% of you think it is still needed; in serious study and research it is a basic skill.
  • Notice your own dependence. If you can't do it once AI is switched off, the way you use it needs adjusting.

For teachers and schools

  • Write the rules down. More than a third of students are unclear about them; clear rules are more useful than vague bans.
  • Teach “how to judge reliability” and “how to ask” directly. These are the two things students asked for most.
  • Give subject-specific guidance rather than a blanket allow or forbid.
  • Design assignments assuming AI exists, for example by asking for the outline, the reasoning, and the checking process.

For parents

  • AI is already an everyday study tool for your child, mostly for understanding and research, but also for getting homework done.
  • A conversation is more useful than a ban: what do they use it for, do they check it, and can they still do it without it?

About this survey

  • Collected through Microsoft Forms from 9 to 13 July 2026; 42 responses.
  • Respondents are secondary-school students invited through personal networks, not a random sample, and mostly Grade 10 boys. All percentages describe these 42 students only.
  • All answers are self-reported.
  • For multiple-choice questions, percentages are the share of respondents who picked each option, so they add up to more than 100%.
  • Grade was a free-text field; it has been grouped into Grades 7–9 and Grades 10, 11, and 12. Two responses that couldn't be placed are shown as “not specified”.
  • Only aggregated data is published here. No personal information is included.
